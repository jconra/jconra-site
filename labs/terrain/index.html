Mountain valley

Mountains on either side of a winding valley floor, with ridged peaks and spurs; the floor falls gently along the valley, so its water runs one way.

Terraces and ledges

Terraces: the height is stepped, a flat top then a short steep riser, in the parts of the land a slow noise picks, each ledge's level nudged so they wander. Erosion: tens of thousands of raindrops run downhill, picking up soil where they speed up and laying it down where they slow, carving gullies and leaving fans of soil at their feet. The steep risers get bare dirt from the mixing, gullies worn dirt, fans damp grass. Changing these rebuilds everything (a second or two).

Erosion and rain

Watching starts from the land before erosion; blue lines are this frame's drops running downhill. The trees and plants come back when it stops.

Cliff faces

Cliff faces: steep ground reads its picture from the side (triplanar, so it is not smeared down the face) and is banded into rock strata.

0b · Water

Found from the land: hollows are filled to the level they would spill at (ponds), and water runs downhill cell by cell, adding up, so rivers form where enough gathers and run into the ponds and out over their spill. Pond ground is flattened to its surface, river channels carved a little; the water is painted on the ground (no see-through mesh) with moving waves, sun glints and the sky reflected.

5 · Mixing by the land

Several ground pictures laid over each other by maps worked out from the land itself, the way terrain tools do it: wet hollows lower than their surroundings, dry rises, forest floor under clumps of trees, lusher grass in the part shade round them, worn paths routed across gentle dry ground, bare steep slopes. Where two meet, the brighter (taller) parts of a picture come through first, so the edge follows the pictures. Noise only roughens the edges. Cost: about a dozen texture reads.

1 · Hex-tiling

The ground is cut into small hexagons. Each gets its own random shift and turn of the texture, and neighbours are blended where they meet, so no two patches line up and the grid of repeats disappears. Cost: three texture reads instead of one.

Sharp edges keep the texture crisp; soft ones blur it. Letting the brighter texture win makes the blend follow the picture (stones over soil) instead of a smooth fade.

2 · Large-scale variation

Big, slow patches of lighter, darker, greener and drier ground laid over everything, the way real ground is never one colour for a whole hillside. Read from noise, so it costs no texture at all.

4 · Stamps

Single leaves, twigs, cones, stones, moss, mushrooms, small plants and grass tufts from your sprite sheets, scattered by the shader: the ground is a grid of cells, and each cell may hold one object at a random spot, turn, size and tint, with a soft shadow. Cost: eight reads of the sprite atlas near the camera; none past the fade distance, and no extra draws.

3 · Far-away second scale

In the distance the same texture is also read at four times the size and blended in, so far ground shows big shapes instead of a fine repeating grain. Cost: one more read (three more with hex-tiling on).

7 · Forest (from the Tree Lab)

The trees are real meshes near you and octahedral imposters beyond a line, crossfading through a band: an imposter is one quad showing the tree as baked from the nearest of many views round it. The atlases are baked in the page from the same trees.

What costs what: a second of frames with each part switched off in turn, from where you are looking.

6 · Ground cover

Your Tripo sheet of 16 plants, split into its plants and scattered by the land's maps: grasses in the open and in part shade, shrubs along the forest's edge and a few inside it, nothing on paths, steep or muddy ground. Near the camera each plant is its real mesh; beyond, an octahedral imposter (8 × 8 views of 128 px over the top half: one quad each), crossfaded, the same system as the trees. Cost: the meshes' triangles near you, a quad each far away.
